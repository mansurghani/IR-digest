Radiofrequency Ablation for Low-Risk T1N0M0 Papillary Thyroid Carcinoma: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.

Korean J Radiol. 2026 Sep 14;27(10):982–1004. doi: 10.3348/kjr.2026.0042

Read the original articlePubMed

Overview

This PROSPERO-registered systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 35 mostly retrospective, single-arm studies (9,477 patients, 9,787 low-risk T1N0M0 papillary thyroid carcinomas) of ultrasound-guided RFA as primary treatment. The pooled complete disappearance rate was 87.2% (95% CI, 81.5%–92.0%; I2 = 97.9%). Disease progression was 1.3% and persistent tumor 0.1%. Total complications were 2.7% and major complications 2 of 9,405 tumors. Volume reduction approached 99% by 36 months. T1b tumors had a much lower complete disappearance rate than T1a (58.8% vs 89.2%). The pooled estimates are descriptive and are limited by very high heterogeneity and the absence of comparator arms.

Study design

Systematic review and meta-analysis, PRISMA-conformant, prospectively registered (PROSPERO CRD420251135714). MEDLINE, EMBASE, Cochrane and KoreaMed were searched from January 2000 to March 2025, English only. Of 2,877 records, 2,140 remained after de-duplication, 123 full texts were assessed and 35 studies were included. Two reviewers screened and extracted data independently. Of the 35 studies, 30 were retrospective and 5 prospective. Six were RFA-versus-surgery comparative studies, and only the RFA arm was extracted. 32 studies were from China, 2 from Korea and 1 from Taiwan. Study periods were 2008–2025. Proportions were pooled with DerSimonian–Laird random effects and Freeman–Tukey transformation. Subgroup differences were tested by meta-regression (T1a vs T1b). Other analyses were exploratory meta-regression, Egger test, leave-one-out sensitivity analysis and RoBANS-2 risk of bias.

Patients

Adults with cytology- or histology-confirmed low-risk PTC (T1a ≤1 cm, T1b >1–2 cm, N0M0) treated with US-guided RFA as primary definitive therapy. Excluded: other ablation modalities without separate RFA data, overlapping cohorts, reviews, case reports and abstracts, and non-English papers. 23 studies enrolled only T1a, 2 only T1b, and 10 mixed T1a/T1b. Only 5 mixed studies reported T1a and T1b outcomes separately. Mean age was 37–66 years, and 62%–91% were women. Mean follow-up was 6–146 months. Mean baseline diameter was 4–14 mm and mean volume 27–1,083 mm3. Most patients were Chinese.

Intervention and comparator

US-guided RFA as primary treatment, single-arm in most studies. Electrode types, power settings, ablation technique and operator experience were not standardized or analyzed. Complete disappearance was defined as absence of the lesion on US or a scar-like lesion, sometimes with contrast-enhanced US or cytology/pathology confirmation. Persistent tumor was viable residual tumor with pre-ablation-like echogenicity and vascularity on early follow-up. Disease progression was the sum of newly developed tumors and lymph node metastasis, and persistent tumors were excluded from it. Major complications were events needing further intervention, surgery or prolonged hospitalization. There was no active comparator in the pooled analysis.

Key figures

Safety

The authors followed the standardized reporting criteria for image-guided tumor ablation. Total complications were 2.7% (95% CI, 1.4%–4.3%). Most were transient, such as mild voice change or localized hematoma, and resolved conservatively. Major complications were 0.02% (2 of 9,405). One permanent voice change was reported (0.01%). Hypothyroidism was listed as an example of a major complication in the methods but was not separately reported. Study-level total complication rates ranged from 0% to 39.3% (Wang 2019 had 39.3%, Luo 2024 had 31.9%), which suggests inconsistent complication definitions and ascertainment. A formal grading system such as SIR or CTCAE was not applied at the pooled level.

Strengths

  • Prospective registration, a four-database search and duplicate independent screening and extraction.
  • Large pooled sample (9,787 tumors), restricted to RFA to avoid mixing modalities.
  • Separates persistent tumor from disease progression and gives a time-resolved VRR trajectory useful for interpreting follow-up imaging.
  • Includes T1a vs T1b subgroup analysis, meta-regression, publication-bias testing and leave-one-out analysis.
  • Transparent about sparse-event methodological issues and about heterogeneity.

Limitations

  • Almost entirely retrospective single-arm data (30 of 35 studies) from China (32 studies), so selection bias and limited generalizability to Western practice. There is no control group, so no inference about equivalence to surgery or active surveillance is possible.
  • Very high, largely unexplained heterogeneity (I2 up to 97.9%). Meta-regression explained less than 10% of variance. Pooled estimates are approximate central tendencies, and prediction intervals, described as wide, are not shown in the text.
  • Outcome definitions are non-standard. Complete disappearance, persistent tumor and progression are defined differently across studies, and disappearance depends on follow-up length. The 87.2% disappearance rate and 0.1% persistent rate do not sum, and 'persistent' was assessed at early follow-up. The 0.1% persistent rate looks implausibly low next to 12.8% non-disappearance, and it may reflect under-reporting or definitional differences.
  • Disease-progression events were summed across heterogeneous follow-up lengths (6–146 months) without time-to-event or person-time analysis. Mean follow-up was not reported in 10 studies, and very few studies had more than 5 years, so durability and late recurrence are poorly characterized.
  • T1b data are sparse (2 T1b-only studies and 5 mixed studies with separate reporting). Confidence intervals are wide (complete disappearance 39.3%–76.9%). Non-significant P values for T1b progression, lymph node metastasis and complications do not demonstrate equivalence. T1b patients were likely selected for technical feasibility. Two of the T1a studies reported a mean diameter of 13.7 mm (Wang 2019) or 6.8 ± 1.9 mm, and tumor-size labeling may be inconsistent.
  • Major-complication and permanent-voice-change estimates rest on 2 and 1 events. Freeman–Tukey degeneracy forced a switch to crude rates and exact CIs, so the forest plots and text differ. The Egger P of 0.003 and the subgroup P values for these outcomes are unreliable.
  • Risk of bias was rated low in 100% of studies for confounding, outcome assessment and blinding. This seems overly optimistic for retrospective single-arm series, and participant selection was unclear in 85.7%. Seven studies were high risk for comparability. Study overlap from the same Chinese centers (several Yan and Xiao publications from 2014–2019) is possible despite exclusion of 9 overlapping cohorts. English-only restriction, no grey literature, and a funnel-plot assessment with few events add further risk.
  • Procedural factors (electrode, power, hydrodissection, operator experience), thyroid function outcomes, quality of life, cost and recurrence-treatment burden were not analyzed. Early VRR calculations in individual studies often showed negative values (early swelling) with huge SDs, so pooled early VRR must be interpreted cautiously.

In context

The authors place the work against earlier meta-analyses that mixed thermal modalities or had short follow-up, and against recent RFA-only meta-analyses that lacked T1a/T1b subgroup analysis and longitudinal VRR data. They cite meta-analyses of RFA vs surgery reporting comparable recurrence-free survival with fewer complications and better thyroid-function preservation. They also note the 2025 ATA guideline acceptance of thermal ablation for low-risk PTC and the new CPT codes. They acknowledge that T1b evidence remains limited and that active surveillance remains a guideline-endorsed option. The new contributions are the RFA-specific scope, the separation of persistent tumor from progression, the T1a vs T1b stratification and the 6–48-month VRR trajectory. These come with the same retrospective, heterogeneous evidence base as the earlier analyses.

Implications for practice

In carefully selected low-risk T1a PTC, mostly in Asian expert centers, RFA appears technically effective, with a low rate of serious complications and rare nodal or new-tumor events during mid-term follow-up. For counseling, the data support an expected course of progressive involution to about 98% volume reduction by 24 months, so residual ablation zones at 6–12 months should not by themselves prompt concern. A persistent or growing lesion should be re-assessed, and biopsy should be considered because some lesions called persistent later prove benign. For T1b disease, complete disappearance is much less common (58.8%) and residual tumor is more frequent (2.2%), so complete ablation-zone coverage, longer imaging follow-up and cautious patient selection are warranted. Confidence is low to moderate. The evidence is observational and heterogeneous and has no comparator, and the authors themselves call the findings hypothesis-generating. These data cannot show RFA is equivalent to surgery or active surveillance. Surveillance should continue for new intrathyroidal tumors, which account for most progression events, and for lymph node metastasis. Prospective, standardized comparative studies with long follow-up are still needed.
